① Why You Don't Need to Worry About Public Restrooms in Korea

The bottom line up front: most public restrooms in Korea are free, and the most reliable ones are in subway stations. You can reach a station restroom without even passing through the turnstiles, so there's no need to buy a ticket or ask staff for permission. Department stores and large malls also have a restroom on every floor, and you usually don't have to be a customer to use one. Worrying about "what if I can't find a restroom" is nearly unfounded within Seoul — the real issue isn't finding the location, it's that the signage and bidet buttons are entirely in Korean.

② The 3-Route Decision Algorithm — The Closest Restroom from Where You Stand

Route When to Use It Characteristics Trap
① Subway station A station within a 5-minute walk Multiple restrooms outside the turnstiles · no ticket needed · free. Major hub stations like Seoul Station, Gangnam, Hongik Univ., Jamsil, and Myeongdong are especially well-maintained At smaller stations, the restroom is sometimes only inside the turnstiles (in that case, go to ②)
② Department store, mall, or large cafe When the subway is too far Follow the floor directory; usually no check on whether you're a customer Large franchise cafes vary by location — some restrict access to paying customers
③ Convenience store When neither ① nor ② is available A quick word to staff: "Hwajangsil jom sseodo doelkkayo? (화장실 좀 써도 될까요?)" — "mind if I use your restroom?" Small stores sometimes have no restroom at all, or staff-only access — being turned down is entirely normal

Decision order: If a subway station is nearby, always go with ① → if not, department store/mall/large cafe ② → if that's also unavailable, ask at a convenience store ③. All three routes failing at once is rare within Seoul.

④ The Bidet-Button Screen Walkthrough — the "Find Stop First" Principle

Most public restrooms in Korea have a bidet attached to the toilet. Layout varies slightly by manufacturer, but the following five buttons show up almost every time.

Icon Label Romanization Function What Happens When Pressed
⏹ Stop (정지) jeongji Stop Immediately halts spraying, drying, and every other function
Wash (세정) sejeong Wash Starts the water spray
Bidet (비데) bide Bidet Sprays from a lower position than Wash
Dry (건조) geonjo Dry Starts warm-air drying
Flush (물내림) mullaerim Flush Automatic or manual flush

Principle: if you panic, don't try to read each button name one by one — find Stop (정지) first. It's usually red, or set apart from the rest of the panel (at the very top or very bottom). Pressing Stop (정지) halts whatever is happening, no matter what — you can take your time checking the other buttons after that.

⑤ Toilet-Paper Culture — the Transition to Flushing It

In the past, Korea followed the same pattern as elsewhere in Asia — used toilet paper went into a bin next to the toilet. In recent years, new and renovated buildings, along with subway stations and department stores, have switched to plumbing that lets you flush toilet paper directly. Older buildings, small shops, and some older-style restrooms, though, still keep a bin inside the stall, often with a sign reading something like "the toilet clogs easily, please put used paper in the bin (변기가 막히니 사용한 휴지는 휴지통에 넣어주세요)."

There's exactly one way to handle this: check for a bin the moment you enter the stall. If there's a bin, follow the sign. If there isn't, flush the paper. The sign itself is the answer, so there's no need to second-guess it.

⑥ A Real-World Walkthrough by Route — Exactly What to Do on the Spot

🚇 Subway station (Route ①)

1. You don't need to pass through the turnstiles — look for the "hwajangsil (화장실) — restroom" pictogram on signage in the concourse (outside the turnstiles)

2. Follow the signage arrows; restrooms are usually placed near elevators and escalators

3. Hub stations like Seoul Station, Gangnam, Hongik Univ., Jamsil, and Myeongdong have more stalls and shorter cleaning intervals

  • Feature: the safest option, usable regardless of whether you've bought a ticket or passed the turnstile

🏬 Department store, mall, or large cafe (Route ②)

1. Check the floor guide near the entrance for a restroom marker — usually at least one per floor

2. Staff rarely check whether you're a paying customer — just walk in

3. Large franchise cafes vary by store: some use a passcode you have to ask the counter for

  • Feature: more pleasant than a subway station, but may take more walking time

🏪 Convenience store (Route ③)

1. A quick word to the register clerk: "Hwajangsil jom sseodo doelkkayo? (화장실 좀 써도 될까요?)"

2. If they say yes, they'll usually direct you to a door beside the staff area or toward the back of the store

3. Being turned down isn't a big deal — small stores often have no restroom at all

  • Feature: the lowest success rate of the three routes, but effectively your only option late at night (see ⑦)

⑦ The Late-Night and Overnight Route

Most subway stations stop operating around midnight, so station restrooms become unreachable after that. Overnight, 24-hour convenience stores (CU, GS25, 7-Eleven, etc. — look for a "24-hour (24시)" sign at the entrance) are essentially your only alternative. Large franchise cafes usually close before midnight, so don't count on them late at night. Rather than walking into just any convenience store overnight, check first for a lit "24-hour" sign, then use the phrase you learned in ② to ask permission.

⑧ 3 Panic Scenarios

1. There's no toilet paper — Bidet Wash (세정) plus Dry (건조) can get you through in a pinch. Nearby convenience stores almost always sell tissue and wet wipes, but the easiest fix is just keeping a small pack of tissues in your bag from the start.

2. You hit the wrong bidet button and water sprays out — Don't panic; press Stop (정지) first, per the principle in ④. If you can't remember where it is, look for the biggest or reddest button on the panel.

3. The line is long — Women's restrooms near weekend tourist spots and concert venues get especially crowded. If another route from ② (department store, convenience store) is nearby, moving to it is often faster than waiting.
