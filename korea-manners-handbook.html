🎯 How to Read This Handbook — The 3 Tags

Most of the panic foreign visitors feel in Korea doesn't come from "not knowing" — it comes from "not knowing you'd gotten it wrong." Can you ask for more banchan (반찬), the side dishes that come with every meal? Can you carry coffee onto the bus? Can you sit in the pink subway seat? Every answer is different. So this handbook tags every single item.

  • [Rule] — Law, ordinance, or official policy. Break it and you get fined or turned away. Sources are at the very bottom.
  • [Custom] — Not law, but almost everyone does it anyway. Skip it and you stand out.
  • [Optional] — Nice to do, fine to skip. Only matters if you want to blend in like a local.

The #number at the end of each item points to the Zero-Panic Manual that breaks that exact situation down screen by screen, action by action. This one page is the map; the manuals are turn-by-turn directions.

⚠ As of September 2026: Two of the [Rule] items are changing right now — a possible reintroduction of the paper-cup regulation at cafes (under review, no start date set) and the designation of no-drinking zones at Hangang Park (한강공원) — still pending before the Seoul city council. Both are marked "changing" in the text below, and this piece gets rechecked within a year of publishing.

① Restaurants — 8 Things

The default setting at a Korean restaurant is "a system built to call staff as little as possible." Utensils live in a drawer, water comes from a dispenser, banchan gets refilled, and you pay at the counter. The customer does the moving instead of the staff — that's what keeps it fast and cheap. Once you understand this structure, you already know half of what a restaurant visit involves (#1168).

  • Utensils & napkins [Custom] — Check the drawer by the table first. Not there? They're standing in a cup somewhere on the table.
  • Calling staff [Custom] — Use the table buzzer. No buzzer? Don't shout — one "Jeogiyo" is all it takes.
  • Self-serve water & banchan [Custom] — A "self-serve water / self-serve banchan" sign on the wall means get it yourself. No sign, no self-serve — use the buzzer.
  • Banchan refills [Custom] — Free at most places. Hold up your empty dish or say "banchan deo juseyo" — more banchan, please. There's no rule that says you have to finish it all.
  • Paying [Custom] — Pay-after at the counter is standard, though prepay kiosks and table-ordering are spreading. A restaurant where you hand a card over at the table is closer to the exception (#1275).
  • Tips [Custom] — None. Even places with a service charge already built in don't expect anything extra on top.
  • Leftovers [Optional] — Many restaurants will pack them up on request. One line covers it: "Nameun geo pojang-hae juseyo" (남은 거 포장해 주세요) — please pack up what's left.
  • Korean BBQ [Custom] — Staff swapping the grill plate or cutting the meat for you is part of the service. Scissors on the table are a normal tool, not a mistake (#1290).

② Cafes — 5 Things

  • Saving a seat [Custom] — Marking a table with a coat, a book, or a bag before you order is standard practice. Some people even leave a wallet or phone behind — travelers shouldn't copy that part.
  • Cups [Rule] — Drink inside and it comes in a mug or glass instead of a disposable plastic cup. Takeout still gets a disposable one. Paper cups are unregulated as of 2026, but a possible reintroduction of the rule means it can vary shop to shop — changing.
  • Buzzers [Custom] — When it buzzes, pick up your order yourself at the counter; straws, napkins, and water are at the self-serve corner (#1288).
  • Camping out [Optional] — One drink per person is the unwritten rule. Whether you can camp at an outlet seat with a laptop for hours depends on the shop — the signage has the answer.
  • Return station [Custom] — Take your empty cup and tray to the return station yourself, and sort ice, leftover liquid, cup, straw, and lid into their separate slots (#1276).

③ Public Transit — 9 Things

  • Pink priority seats [Custom] — Leaving the pink seats empty, even with nobody around, is an unwritten rule. There's no fine for sitting there, but most locals leave it open anyway.
  • Priority seating [Custom] — Same goes for the seats at both ends of the car. Standing even on a crowded train is the natural move.
  • Backpacks [Optional] — On a packed subway, wear it on your front or carry it in your hand.
  • Calls & sound [Custom] — Playing video without earphones or taking a loud call earns you the nickname "billeon" (빌런) — villain (#1169). Keep calls short and quiet.
  • Drinks on buses [Rule] — Seoul city buses ban open takeout cups and smelly food on board (a Seoul city ordinance, in effect since 2018). The driver can refuse to let you on. A sealed tumbler or airtight container is fine.
  • Food on the subway [Custom] — No ordinance bans it like on buses — but nobody actually eats smelly food down there.
  • Escalators [Custom] — In practice it's a single line on the right, walkers pass on the left. The official stance recommends two lines for safety but doesn't enforce it — just do what everyone else does.
  • Getting off the bus [Custom] — Press the buzzer, exit through the back door, and tag your card again on the way out for the transfer discount (#1271).
  • Taxis [Custom] — Doors open and close by hand, not automatically. The front seat is fine to sit in. No tipping — take your change as given (#1273).

④ Street & Public Spaces — 7 Things

  • Trash cans [Custom] — Almost never on the street. Carry it with you to a subway station's recycling bin or a convenience store's trash can, as a paying customer (#1276).
  • Smoking [Rule] — Smoking while walking down the street is subject to enforcement, and smoking within 10m of a bus stop or in a designated no-smoking zone brings a fine of up to ₩100,000. Smoking booths and designated zones only (#1330).
  • Foot traffic [Optional] — Follow the "keep right" arrows if you see them on stairs or in passageways.
  • Drinking in parks [Custom · changing] — Cracking open a beer at Hangang Park — what's loosely called chimaek (치맥) culture here — is allowed as of September 2026. But Seoul is pushing to designate no-drinking zones (currently pending before the city council), so this could change. Being disruptive or leaving trash behind is a separate issue regardless.
  • No-kids / no-pet zones [Custom] — Check the entrance sign first. If it's posted, kids and pets aren't allowed inside. It's a contested policy, but a shop's own rule is respected.
  • Elevators [Custom] — Hitting the close-door button right away isn't rude — it's just the default move. See #1143 on ppalli-ppalli (빨리빨리), Korea's "hurry-hurry" culture.
  • Fine dust [Optional] — Masks on "bad" air-quality days in spring and winter are a common sight. Check an app like AirKorea to be sure.

⑤ Lodging — 6 Things

Panic clusters around the first night at an Airbnb, a guesthouse, or a hanok (한옥) stay. If there's an instruction sheet posted, it overrides this handbook.

  • Shoes [Custom] — Off at the entrance, then indoor slippers. True at ondol-heated lodging and floor-seating restaurants alike (#1141).
  • Boiler panel [Custom] — Three buttons on the wall panel matter: [ Heating ], [ Hot Water ], [ Away ]. No hot water? Check the hot-water mode first. Floor too hot? Turn down the heating.
  • Trash [Rule] — General trash goes only into a jongnyangje bongtu (종량제 봉투) — a pay-per-volume trash bag. The bags are sold at convenience stores and marts; prices and food-waste procedures vary by district, so the lodging's own instructions take priority.
  • Food waste [Rule] — Kept separate from general trash, using whichever system the lodging has: a dedicated bag, a collection bin, or an RFID-metered scale.
  • Noise [Optional] — Watch the hallway noise, footsteps, and washing machine after 10pm — apartment-style lodging is especially sensitive to noise between floors.
  • Toilet paper [Custom] — Since 2018, most places let you flush it. If an older building has a sign asking you to use the trash bin instead, follow that (#1277).

⑥ The 5 Drinking-Table Moves — In One Box

The "why" behind office dinners and soju culture lives in #1140. Here are the five moves a traveler actually needs.

1. Receive your glass with both hands (if you only have one free, rest the other on your elbow).

2. Pour with both hands too, bottle label facing up.

3. In front of someone older, turn your head slightly to the side while you drink — no need to overdo it.

4. Top off only an empty glass. Don't pour into one that's still partly full.

5. Can't drink any more? Just leave your glass full and say "Gwaenchanayo" (괜찮아요) — I'm fine. People push much less than they used to.

⑦ Conversation — 3 Lines

  • People you don't know by name get called "sajangnim, imo-nim, jeogiyo" — boss, auntie, excuse-me. Someone you just met asking your age is about settling on the right form of address, not rudeness (#1150).
  • Formal versus casual speech isn't expected from a foreign visitor. A short greeting and a smile are enough.
  • There's a knack for avoiding directness called nunchi (눈치) (#1145) — "geulsseyo," well now, usually means "no."

⑧ Shopping & Payment — 3 Things

  • Cash [Custom] — Barely needed. Markets, a few old-school shops, and buses that won't take cash at all are covered in #1301.
  • Self-checkout [Custom] — Convenience-store self-checkout counters and unstaffed night stores let you in with a card tag (#1306).
  • Tax refund [Rule] — Instant-refund shops and the airport kiosk (#1304).

⑨ Photos & Temples — 3 Things

  • Inside shops, and people [Custom] — Ask first before photographing merchandise, staff, or other customers at cafes and markets. Selfies are fair game.
  • Restricted areas [Rule] — Military facilities, airport security zones, and some museum galleries follow the posted signs. The inside of a beopdang is often off-limits for photos.
  • Temple etiquette [Custom] — Shoes and hats off in front of the beopdang, and keep quiet (#1186 · #1137).

🙆 7 Things You Don't Need to Do — Undoing the Over-Politeness

1. Tipping — None.

2. Opening the taxi door yourself — the driver won't either. Everyone opens their own.

3. A deep bow — a light nod is plenty. Nobody expects a full bow from a visitor.

4. Finishing every banchan — leaving some is fine. It's actually the norm.

5. Draining your glass — a one-shot isn't mandatory.

6. Calling ahead to book a restaurant — apps, tablets, and walking in to wait are the default (#1274). No phone number doesn't mean you can't go.

7. Perfectly formal speech — not expected from a foreign visitor. "Gamsahamnida" — thank you — is enough.

⚠ 5 Common Mistakes

1. Sitting in a pink seat — leave it empty even when no one's around. Sitting there draws stares.

2. Boarding a bus with an open coffee cup — Seoul can refuse you by ordinance. Close the lid or finish the drink before you get on.

3. Smoking while walking — a fine applies within 10m of a stop or in a no-smoking zone. Find a booth instead (#1330).

4. Leaving a tip behind — staff will chase you down to give it back. Say "jal meogeosseumnida" — that was delicious — instead.

5. Tossing lodging trash in any old bag — if it's not the jongnyangje bag, it won't get collected.

⚙ 5 FAQ

1. Do I only need to follow the [Rule] items? — Fines only come from [Rule]. But "blending in like a local" is decided by [Custom]. [Optional] is just that — optional.

2. What about a tumbler on the bus? — A sealed tumbler, thermos, or airtight container is fine. A canned drink with a straw in it or an open cup is the problem.

3. What if I stand on the left on an escalator? — Whoever's walking behind you waits. Not against any rule, but standing on the right is just easier.

4. Can I drink beer at Hangang Park? — Yes, as of September 2026. A no-drinking-zone designation is in progress, so check the posted signage whenever you actually visit.

5. What if a cafe gives me a mug but I need to leave? — Tell the counter and they'll transfer it into a disposable cup.
